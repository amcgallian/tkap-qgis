Section Drawing

Draws sections over a rectified photo of the wall.

Overview

A photo of an excavation wall is rectified into a section-local coordinate space where x is chainage along the section trace and y is absolute elevation. Because y is elevation, the elevation axis on the finished figure is just a map grid, and every polygon you draw is directly comparable to the RTK elevations in the database.

Drawing happens on the main QGIS canvas, not in a dialog, so you get the real vertex tool, snapping, undo and layouts. Everything the tool changes (project CRS, layer visibility, snapping) is recorded and restored when you finish.

Output is two figures: a clean measured drawing, and a wireframe over the photo. Both carry an elevation axis.

Newer than the other two tools. Treat it as beta.

Tutorial

Load and filter your SU layer

Set the project up as you normally would. The tool reads whatever layer you point it at, honouring its filter and its symbology. PostGIS, file GDB and shapefile all work.

Draw the trace

Plugins → TKAP Tools → Draw a section…, then click one end of the wall and the other in plan view.

While you drag, the preview shows three things: the shaded band is the face being drawn, the circle marks chainage 0, and the arrow shows which way chainage grows, which is also the direction the finished drawing reads left to right.

Press SPACE or F to put the face on the other side. Right-click cancels.

Which side you're looking at decides both which SUs get collected and whether the photo comes out mirrored. Pick it here, where the shaded band and arrow make it obvious, rather than finding out from a reversed drawing later.

Set up the section

  • Stratigraphic units tab: choose the SU layer, pick SUs either by crossing the trace or by their space (PostGIS only), and review the seed each one will get.
  • Photo & control points tab: load the wall photo, load the Emlid CSV, click each control point on the image, and check the fit.

Check the datum before you trust an elevation

The control points and the database aren't necessarily on the same vertical datum. A file recorded on the receiver's "Global CS" has an empty Elevation column and only ellipsoidal height filled, at values around 1066 m, while altitude_min/altitude_max for the same wall read about 1030 m. The difference is the geoid separation, roughly 35.9 m at this site.

Heights on the drawing decides which of the two the section is drawn in. It defaults to ellipsoidal, because that is the height the receiver actually measures: an orthometric height is only as good as the geoid model behind it, and a receiver carrying the wrong model hands over a plausible-looking height that is quietly wrong. Switch it to orthometric when you want the figure labelled with the same numbers as the SU table.

Whichever you choose, everything is converted onto it — the control points, and the recorded SU altitudes the seed boxes come from — so the photo and the boxes always agree. Gap between the two is the separation that conversion uses, and Work it out from a known height solves for it: enter the orthometric elevation of the top of the wall and it compares that against the highest control point.

Get it wrong and the photo sits 36 m above the SUs, so the pre-flight checklist states which datum the heights ended up on, and objects when heights arrive on the other one with the gap left at 0.

PROJ won't do this for you here: no geoid grid is installed, and a vertical transform to EGM2008 returns the height unchanged rather than failing.

Start drawing

The project switches into section-local coordinates, plan layers hide, the photo appears with seeded SU boxes over it, and the layer goes into edit mode. Vertex tool, reshape, split and snapping all behave as usual.

Seeds are full-height columns by default: each SU is placed by its chainage extent along the wall and stretched over the height of the section, stopping short of each edge so the edges are grabbable and read as provisional. You drag the top and bottom to where the photo says they belong.

Recorded elevations still show in the SU table, and Seed from recorded SU elevations where available opts into using them for a section where the data is known good.

Manage the roster as you go

ControlWhat it does
ShowHides a unit without removing it.
IsolateShows only the selected unit.
Add SU…Brings in units that were missed, either by widening the search buffer or by picking a number from anywhere in the layer.
Re-seedReplaces a unit's polygon with a fresh seed box.
RemoveTakes a unit out of the section entirely, polygon and row. Add unit… brings it back.

Unticking Show renders a unit with a transparent symbol rather than filtering it out, so it keeps snapping. You can hide a crowded neighbour and still snap a new boundary onto its edge. Hidden units still appear in the exported figures; use Remove to exclude something.

Crop the frame to what you want in the figure

The dashed cyan box is the section frame, and it is exactly what the exported figure covers. Press Resize on canvas in the panel and drag its corners or edges; the drawing follows. Escape, or the button again, gives the normal tools back.

This is the way to deal with an ortho that covers more wall than the section. Rather than cropping the raster and re-rectifying, pull the frame in to the part you want and export. The scale, page size and elevation axis all recompute from the cropped frame.

The four boxes under it take exact chainage and elevation limits if you need the section to end at a stated value. Fit to photo snaps the frame to the placed photo, and Reset chainage puts it back to the full trace you drew. A cropped frame is kept when you save and reopen.

Resizing the frame changes what the figure covers and nothing else. Which units the section holds is decided at setup and changed only by Add unit… and Remove, so cropping never adds or takes away rows behind your back.

Name units, if a number isn't the useful name

The panel's Label column takes free text for any unit — rocky fill, collapse, whatever the drawing should call it. Leave it empty and the unit keeps its number.

The Shows column next to it decides what the drawing writes on that unit: Number, Label, Number and label, or Nothing. It's set per unit, so one section can carry numbers throughout, name the two or three units worth naming, and leave a thin lens blank where a number wouldn't fit.

Label falls back to the number when no label has been typed, so a unit is never accidentally blank — use Nothing when you mean blank.

Export

From the panel, export the clean drawing, the wireframe over the photo, or both. PNG, PDF and SVG.

The dialog sets page size and orientation, margin, scale (fitted or fixed to a round value), the height-line interval, legend, scale bar, frame and resolution — with a live preview of the real layout, not a mock-up.

It also sets whether units are labelled at all and at what size (what each one says is per unit, above), the caption under the title (typed by hand, left off, or the automatic “Looking north · 1:20”), and for the wireframe the outline colour, outline width, backdrop and how far to fade the photo.

Fading the photo is what makes the backdrop visible: a placed ortho is axis-aligned and fills the frame, so at full strength there is nothing behind it to see. Raise the fade and the photo knocks back towards the backdrop colour, which is usually how you get yellow outlines to read over a bright sunlit wall.

Every control redraws the preview as you change it, so what you see is the real layout rather than a mock-up. Resolution is the one exception — the preview renders at its own.

Your choices are remembered per output kind, so the next export starts where the last one left off. The title and caption aren't remembered, since they name one particular section.

Finish

Restores your project CRS, layer visibility and snapping.

Saving and reopening

Save in the panel writes the section to a GeoPackage. Plugins → TKAP Tools → Open a saved section… reads one back and carries on editing.

The rectified photo is referenced, not stored — an ortho is tens of megabytes and folding one into every section file would make them unwieldy. But saving puts a copy of it beside the section file, as <section>_photo.tif, and records the path both relative and absolute. Copy the pair to a share or another machine and the backdrop travels with the drawing. Reopening looks in four places, in falling order of how much each one proves: beside the section file, the path it was saved from, a matching _photo.tif in the same folder, and finally the original photograph — which it can place again by itself, because the transform and the control points behind it are saved too. It tells you which one it used.

When none of them work, Re-link photo… under Data sources points the section back at its photo. Hand it the rectified GeoTIFF and it loads as it stands; hand it the original photograph and it re-places it from the saved transform. It checks the image is the size that transform was measured against and refuses if it isn't, because the transform maps pixels and a re-cropped copy would put the drawing somewhere on the wall it never was. Sections drawn before v1.5.0 have no saved placement, so for those the button opens the control-point picker instead — with the trace, the flip, the datum and the extent all fixed, since the units are already drawn against them.

The SU layer is referenced the same way. A saved section records which layer it was drawn from and looks it up on reopen — by id, then by data source, then by name — so a rebuilt project or a renamed layer still finds it. When it can't, the section still opens with everything you drew, and Data sources in the panel lets you point it at the right layer. That also repoints the symbology and the export, and it can be changed at any time, not just on reopen.

Notes

  • The section CRS is a transverse mercator with lat_0 = lon_0 = 0 and unit scale. The trace parameters are written into the CRS REMARK, so any layer or raster carrying it can be tied back to EPSG:32636.
  • The fit reports when a placement comes out mirrored, with the right of the photo landing on the left of the drawing, and offers a one-click flip.
  • One survey file can cover several walls. Points are selected by perpendicular distance to the trace, and the dialog says how many were left out.
  • When picking control points, zoom in as far as you like: the full-resolution pixels for the region in view are read on demand and drawn over the fast overview, so a nailhead resolves into real pixels instead of the downsampled blur it used to be. Only the region you are looking at is held at full resolution, so this does not get slower on a larger ortho.
